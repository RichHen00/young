A stubborn refusal to accept the death of sea travel leads Gavin Young into the imperial world of the Swires. Among ship’s bells resembling warriors' skulls, the author encounters a vanishing era of maritime grandeur and colonial power.

Not encouraging. But there must, I thought, be some way, however erratic, to travel by sea to Asia. I refused to believe it was impossible.

A call to Swire & Sons, one of the biggest British trading and shipping companies in the Far East, brought an invitation from John Swire, head of the giant British group that includes, among much else, the China Navigation Company in Hong Kong.

Swire’s is big and grand. Grand enough and old enough to have accumulated traditions and a book or two of imperial history. Beautiful, meticulously constructed models of Swire ships past and present stood along the walls in glass cases. A collection of old ship’s bells lay in a row like the skulls of warriors in an African burial cave. I was looking at a bell inscribed with the name Hupeh and the date 1937 when John Swire, a towering, soldierly figure, came up.

— Slow Boats to China, GAVIN YOUNG
